*This article is general information about US rules, not legal advice. Consent rules depend on what you sell, where your leads live and how you call them. Have a lawyer review your forms and your calling process.*

If you call the people who fill in your forms, the wording next to the submit button matters. In the US, some kinds of calls need the person's prior express written consent, and the form is usually where that consent is given or missed. This guide explains what the term means, what a consent line should cover and what records to keep.

## Why it matters for calling leads

The main US law here is the Telephone Consumer Protection Act (TCPA), with rules set by the Federal Communications Commission (FCC). Under those rules, telemarketing calls to mobile numbers that use an autodialer or an artificial or prerecorded voice need the person's prior express written consent.

Two points make this relevant to almost any business that calls its leads:

- **Most leads give a mobile number.** That is the number people put on forms.
- **AI voices count as artificial voices.** In February 2024 the FCC ruled that calls using AI-generated voices fall under the TCPA's rules on artificial voices ([FCC announcement](https://www.fcc.gov/document/fcc-makes-ai-generated-voices-robocalls-illegal)). If you use an AI voice agent to call leads, plan on needing the same consent as for a prerecorded call.

The law allows people to sue for each call made without the required consent, which is why the form wording deserves ten minutes of attention.

## What "prior express written consent" means

The definition is in the FCC's rules ([47 CFR 64.1200](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-64/subpart-L/section-64.1200)). In plain terms, it is a written agreement, signed by the person (an electronic signature such as ticking a box and submitting a form can count, under the E-SIGN Act), that:

- clearly authorises **your business** to call them,
- covers calls made with an autodialer or an artificial or prerecorded voice,
- names the **phone number** they want to be called on, and
- tells them that agreeing is **not a condition of buying** anything.

The disclosure has to be clear and conspicuous. Consent hidden in a long terms page, or given by a box that was ticked before they arrived, is the kind of thing that fails.

## What a consent line on a form looks like

Here is an example of the elements, using a made-up business name, to adapt with your lawyer rather than copy as is:

> ☐ By ticking this box and submitting this form, I agree that Brightline Roofing may call and text me at the number I entered, including with automated technology and AI or prerecorded voices, about my enquiry and related services. Consent is not a condition of purchase. Message and data rates may apply. I can opt out at any time.

What makes it work:

- **It names your business.** Not "our partners", not a list of unnamed companies.
- **It names the technology.** Automated calls and AI or prerecorded voices, in words a person understands.
- **It is next to the submit button,** not on a separate page.
- **The box starts unticked,** and the person ticks it themselves.
- **It says consent is optional** for buying.

Some businesses make the box required for the call-back option and offer another way to get in touch, such as email, for people who do not want calls.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Pre-ticked boxes.** The person has to take the action.
- **Consent buried in the terms of service.** If they cannot see it before submitting, assume it does not count.
- **Consent for someone else.** If a lead form names one business, it does not cover calls from another, including one you share leads with.
- **Bought leads you cannot trace.** If you buy leads, you need to know exactly what the person agreed to and for whom. If you cannot show it, do not call them with an AI or prerecorded voice.
- **Changing the wording without a record.** If you cannot show what the form said on the day someone submitted it, the consent is hard to prove.

## Records to keep

For each lead, keep enough to show what they agreed to:

- the date and time of the submission,
- the page or form it came from,
- the exact consent wording shown at the time (keep a dated copy of each version),
- the phone number they entered, and
- technical details your form tool captures, such as the IP address.

Most form tools and CRMs store some of this automatically. Check that yours keeps the consent wording version, which is the piece most often missing.

## Calling hours

FCC rules restrict telemarketing calls to between 8am and 9pm in the recipient's local time, and some states set narrower windows or extra limits. Decide your calling window with that in mind, and remember that the lead's time zone may differ from yours.

## Opt-outs

People can withdraw consent. The FCC's rules say consumers can revoke consent by any reasonable means, such as replying "stop" or telling the caller they do not want more calls, and businesses must honour that promptly. Make sure an opt-out recorded on a call or a text actually stops the next call.

## State laws

Several states have their own telemarketing laws with stricter rules on consent, calling hours or the number of calls. If your leads are concentrated in particular states, check those states' rules too.

None of this is a reason to call slowly. A person who just submitted a form with clear consent and a request for a call usually wants that call now. Getting the form right is what lets you call them straight away with confidence.
